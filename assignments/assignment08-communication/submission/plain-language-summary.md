# Plain-Language Summary

**Main point:** In one classroom NHANES dataset, adults aged 20-80 with low family income had a slightly higher average BMI in recent survey years. This does not show what caused the rise.

This summary is for adults who see an online article or post saying that low income leads to weight gain.

Low family income here means income below 1.3 times the poverty line, the least income the U.S. government says a family needs for basic living. In this group, average body mass index (BMI) was 28.8 in 1999-2000 and 30.4 in 2021-2023. That is a rise of under 2 units over 11 survey cycles, with a few small dips.

This result has limits. BMI does not fit every body. For example, athletes can have a high BMI from muscle, not poor health. The data describe only the people in this classroom dataset, not all Americans. Each survey cycle also measured different people, so the line does not show the same people gaining weight. The chart cannot show that low income causes higher BMI. For example, getting older could also raise BMI.

If you see a post saying low income causes weight gain, treat it with caution, because this data cannot prove that. A useful next step would be to compare other income groups to see how average BMI differs by income.

Source: Prepared classroom snapshot derived from CDC/NCHS NHANES public-use data. Results are descriptive and unweighted.
